Why progress photos beat the scale

The scale measures everything at once: fat, muscle, water, glycogen, last night's salty dinner. That makes it noisy in the short term. In a study of 1,421 adults, weight rose over the weekend and fell during the week as a normal pattern,1 and a Finnish study of daily weigh-ins concluded the same weekly rhythm should be seen as normal, not as weight gain.2

The bigger blind spot is body composition. In a four-week trial combining a calorie deficit, training and a higher-protein diet, participants gained 1.2 kg of lean mass while losing 4.8 kg of fat.3 The scale showed 3.6 kg. The body changed by six. That's the change photos capture and numbers miss: shoulders, posture, waist, the way a T-shirt sits.

  • The scalePrecise, frequent and noisy. Great for long-term trends, poor at telling fat from water or muscle.
  • PhotosShow shape and proportions. Only useful if every photo is taken the same way.
  • How you feelEnergy, sleep, clothes fitting. The measure most worth listening to, and the one no app can take for you.

How to take progress photos you can actually compare

A progress photo is only as good as its consistency. Change the distance, the angle or the light, and you're comparing photography, not bodies.

  1. Same place, same light. Pick one spot with even, front-facing light. Daylight from a window in front of you works well. Avoid overhead lights that carve shadows.
  2. Same time and conditions. Morning, before eating, in similar clothes. Food, water and a pump after training all change how you look.
  3. Same distance and height. Prop the phone at chest height, far enough away that your head and feet both fit. A timer beats a mirror selfie every time.
  4. Same poses. Front and side at minimum, back if you can. Relaxed, arms slightly away from your body, no sucking in.
  5. Same rhythm. Every week or two is plenty. Daily photos show mostly noise, and can turn into checking.

This is exactly where most apps stop helping. Getting steps three and four right by yourself, week after week, is hard. It's the part we built Fimbl's camera around.

Privacy: where your body photos live

Progress photos are among the most personal pictures on your phone, often taken in underwear or less. Where they're stored matters. In 2025, researchers found a fitness app's cloud storage left open to anyone, exposing more than 138,000 users' progress photos.10 It was closed after it was reported, but the lesson stands: a photo that's never uploaded can't leak from a server.

What about AI body-fat scans?

Some apps now estimate body-fat percentage from photos or a short video. Noom's Body Scan, for example, uses technology from Prism Labs and sends the scan for processing.11, 12 The idea is appealing, but the numbers are rough: an independent study of two smartphone apps found typical errors of 3.9 to 6.2 percentage points of body fat compared with a lab reference.13 That's the difference between 22% and 28%. For tracking your own change, consistent photos and a weight trend tell you more, and they don't require uploading pictures of your body.

Photos should make you feel more informed, not more critical. Repeatedly checking your body can increase body dissatisfaction, especially for people who already worry about how they look.14 Take photos every week or two rather than every day, look at them alongside how you feel, and pause if they start to make you anxious. If thoughts about your body or food take up much of your day, a doctor or an eating-disorder service can help. Fimbl isn't a medical tool or a treatment for eating disorders.
